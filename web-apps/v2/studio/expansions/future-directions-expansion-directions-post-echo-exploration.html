Expansion Directions — Post-Echo Exploration

Fresh exploration under the revised product model. The previous directions document is archived: three of its four ideas spent Echo as an expansion asset, and Echo is now spoken for.

The product model this assumes

  • Base game: 2–4 players. Four energies × 1–15, + 2 wilds.

  • Extension (Catan-style — more players, same game): Echo 1–15 + 2 wilds, enabling 5–6 players. Echo is a full peer suit, mechanically identical to the other four. This box also carries the parked Stage-4+ enchantment-ladder idea if it survives testing — but it is an extension, not a design-space vehicle.

  • Existing expansions: Ascension Trials (owns evaluation) and Spell Duels (owns player collision, tiered).

The compatibility test every direction below must pass

An expansion must play identically at 2–4 players (no Echo in the deck) and at 5–6 players (Echo extension in). No expansion may require Echo, reference Echo by name in a core rule, or behave differently depending on suit count — at most it may scale the way the base game already scales (per-player components; a fifth copy of anything suit-indexed lives in the extension box, mirroring how Spell Duels handles Echo strikes with one bolt-on ruling.)

Practical consequences:

  • Suit-agnostic wording everywhere: rules say "an energy of your choice / the same energy / each energy in your Spellbook," never a named suit list. Then four suits or five, nothing changes.

  • The 16+ value space survives the model change — and gets cleaner: values are now 1–15 at every count, so "16 and above" is unambiguous, one printed set, no dual-deck variants. It remains the one parked design-space asset still available to expansions.

  • The design-space register is now: Echo → spent (extension). 16+ values → available. Everything else must come from new components or new use of existing structures (the Drought, the Reserve, the Array, player identity, the mode of play).

The Catan test still applies: Trials owns how the game scores, Duels owns how players collide. Each direction below must own a third throughline.

Direction 1 — The Overtones

Throughline: vertical prestige — the big box. Spends the 16+ space.

Hook. Beyond the fifteenth frequency lie the overtones — resonances the Academy sealed because no living wizard could hold them. They cannot be drawn from the Source. They sit in a visible Overtone rack from setup, each behind a claim condition, and every game becomes — in part — a public race up the ladder.

Mechanical sketch.

  • A rack of components valued 16–20, four per base energy, displayed face-up. Claim conditions are expressed purely in base verbs: cast a 6+ Conjuration → claim the lowest overtone of its energy; complete a Perfect Transmutation → claim any overtone of its energy; hold three Enchantments → claim from any energy. Claiming is free and immediate when the condition fires.

  • Overtones extend pattern space upward: a 14-15-16 Transfiguration, a PT running 16-17-18, matches at ranks that have only 1 copy per suit until claimed (i.e., overtone Enchantments are near-legendary by supply).

  • Masterworks (spells containing 2+ overtones) score on a new tier; with Trials, one new trial — The Deep Resonance (masterwork power only) — slots into the existing extra-trial pattern.

  • No new phase, no new action, no new verb.

Compatibility test: passes by construction — conditions are suit-agnostic and the rack is four energies at 2–4p. The extension box carries Echo 16–20, and the rack simply gains a fifth column at 5–6p. Nothing else changes.

Complexity vs. the pair: the heaviest direction — at or above Spell Duels Tier 4. All the weight is legible, though: the rack is public, aspirational, and self-teaching ("what do those big numbers do?" is the first question every new player will ask).

Why distinct / worth building: nothing in the line is aspirational during play — Duels is horizontal, Trials is retrospective. A visible prestige race gives the mid-game a public arc, gives strong players a ceiling, and makes the game's own numbers the treasure. It is also the natural flagship: the direction that most changes what a full evening arcs toward.

Direction 2 — The Schools

Throughline: player identity and replayability — the light box.

Hook. No two wizards were trained the same way. Each player begins as a graduate of a School — Scribes, Wardens, Chronomancers, Ashbinders… — with one small always-on talent and one signature scoring lean. Same game, different wizard every night.

Mechanical sketch.

  • A deck of School cards; deal 2 to each player at setup, keep 1 (a draft, so identity is a choice, not a stroke of luck).

  • Each School = one talent + one lean, both expressed in suit-agnostic base verbs:

    • Talent examples: "your 3-card Transfigurations exchange discard-1-take-1 (as if 4-card)"; "once per turn you may Reshape as a free action"; "when you Unlearn, keep one component playable immediately"; "you may hold your Collection pick until after Casting."

    • Lean examples: "+2 RP per Transfiguration at evaluation"; "+1 RP per distinct energy in your Spellbook"; "your largest spell scores one row higher."

  • Hard design rules: no talent touches capacity/counters (the protected economy); no talent names an energy (compatibility test); every talent must be checkable at a glance (printed on the card, zero memory).

  • With Trials: each School may also carry a one-line trial affinity ("in Energy Harmony ties, you win"). With Duels: optional duel quirks per School are a natural cross-sell, gated behind both boxes.

Compatibility test: passes if the no-named-energy rule holds; player count is irrelevant to per-player identity cards. 5–6p just deals from the same deck.

Complexity vs. the pair: the lightest thing in the whole line — below Spell Duels Tier 1 in rules weight (one card per player, self-teaching). Its cost is invisible: balance across Schools is the entire development effort, and asymmetry multiplies every future balance question (each new module must be checked against each School).

Why distinct / worth building: it owns who you are — a dimension neither scoring nor conflict touches. It's the classic second-purchase box (cheap to print, huge replayability-per-component), and it's the only direction that changes the first five minutes of every game rather than the last twenty. Also the best candidate to develop early, because Schools give playtesters a reason to return to the base loop repeatedly — free testing mileage.

Direction 3 — The Last Rites

Throughline: the Drought as a third act — no one owns the ending yet.

Hook. When the Source dies, magic doesn't stop — it changes state. The Released Reserve is not a heap of dead cards; it is the world's spent magic, unstable and singing. Rites — desperate, end-times workings that only function in a dying world — become possible exactly when ordinary casting fails.

Mechanical sketch (deliberately Echo-free — the archived version needed the fifth suit; this one uses the Reserve itself).

  • A small deck of Rite cards, face-up from game start (so players build toward them all game), but performable only during the Drought.

  • Performing a Rite = paying its cost from your Spellbook or hand into the Released Reserve — feeding your own built magic back to the dying world — for a scoring or manipulation effect: bind your largest spell (it can't be changed, +RP); pull any 3 cards from the Released Reserve discard-history; convert a spell to a different type at evaluation; add a fifth turn of Drought for everyone (once).

  • Because Rites consume Spellbook components, the Drought becomes a real decision space: strip your engine for Rites, or hold your patterns for standard evaluation — the disassembly game the current Drought never asks for.

  • Rites are public and slot-limited (each Rite performable once per game, first come first served) — a closing race with the same public-arc quality as Direction 1, but compressed into the endgame.

Compatibility test: passes — the Reserve, the Drought, and suit-agnostic costs ("any 4 components of one energy") exist identically at every count. At 5–6p the Reserve is simply bigger and the Drought longer, which Rite costs can scale with (player-count-indexed costs printed on the cards, the same way Trials RP pools already scale).

Complexity vs. the pair: ≈ Trials — near-zero weight for two acts, one concentrated learning moment at the trigger. Natural partner to Trials (Rites reshape what the Trials evaluate) and fully compatible with Duels (duels are off in the Drought; no overlap at all).

Why distinct / worth building: the Drought is the game's title moment and its flattest stretch — every "the ending is quieter than the build-up" note is this box's market research. The archived exploration reached the same target via Echo; this route is better even ignoring the model change, because it works at 2 players and turns the endgame's existing components into the content instead of adding a suit.

Direction 4 — The Convergence

Throughline: co-op and solo — a different audience, not a different mechanism.

Hook. Before the magic chooses an Archmage, it tests whether the Academy deserves one. The Convergence is the game with the dying magic as the opponent: satisfy the world's demands together before the Source collapses, or nobody ascends.

Mechanical sketch.

  • A dedicated Convergence deck (its own cards — the archived version used Echo as the NPC voice; a purpose-built deck is cleaner and count-proof). Each card shows a demand pattern in the game's own pattern language: "a 4-length sequence spanning 3+ energies," "matched triples at two different ranks," "any Perfect Transmutation," each with a deadline expressed in Source depth.

  • Meeting a demand = players binding components from their Spellbooks to it (committed, not spent — bound spells still stand but can't be Reshaped). Failure = banish cards from the Source (the clock tightens — the same lever Spell Duels Tier 4 gives players, held by the game).

  • Difficulty scales by demands-per-round and deadline depth, indexed to player count 1–6 — this is the only box that plays solo.

  • Endgame: a cooperative Trials variant — the table pools power into final Convergence trials; group score is the metric.

Compatibility test: passes — demands are written in suit-agnostic pattern language ("3+ different energies" is satisfiable with four suits and merely easier terrain with five; the difficulty index for 5–6p accounts for it).

Complexity vs. the pair: medium rules weight (above Trials, under Duels Tier 4); development cost is a different discipline — difficulty curves rather than fairness — which argues for building it after at least one competitive expansion has taught the production pipeline.

Why distinct / worth building: the only direction aimed at players the line structurally cannot serve today (solo; co-op tables). Commercially the standard third leg of a modern line; thematically free, since the depleting Source is already the villain.

Sequencing observation (not a commitment): Schools is the cheapest to prototype and pays for itself in testing mileage; Last Rites attacks the game's known weakest beat; Overtones is the destination big-box; Convergence is the audience-expander best built once the pipeline exists. A plausible line: Schools → Last Rites → Overtones → Convergence — but nothing here is Stage-3 work, and none of it moves before the counter fix and the Echo extension itself are validated.

One dependency to protect: the compatibility test above should be added to any future expansion brief as a standing rule — it is what keeps the extension a true Catan-style extension (more players, same game) instead of a hidden expansion dependency.

Part 2 — How the four work together (added 2026-07-02)

The pairwise matrix. "Clean" = no rules contact, coexist freely. "Synergy" = each makes the other better. "Contact" = a rule must say something. "Tension" = watch it.

SchoolsLast RitesOvertonesConvergence
Schools—Synergy + tensionSynergySynergy
Last Rites—Contact (good)Contact (redesign)
Overtones—Contact (good)
Convergence—

  • Schools × Last Rites — the natural first pairing. Schools shape what you build; Rites decide what you sacrifice at the end — identity in act one, identity crisis in act three. One genuine tension: any School lean that rewards holding cards (Archivists) stacks with any Rite that converts hand to points (Rite of Ash). Rule of thumb adopted in both rulesets: a School lean and a Rite may never reward the same resource — the rosters below were checked against each other line by line.

  • Schools × Overtones. Free synergy: claim conditions are School-shaped ("complete a PT" is trivially a Chronomancer goal), so Schools give the Overtone race asymmetric routes — the big box gets better if Schools exist first. No rules contact at all.

  • Schools × Convergence. Co-op is where asymmetric identity shines most (each player owns a role). Zero rules contact; Schools is the one competitive box that drops into co-op unchanged.

  • Last Rites × Overtones. One contact point, and it's delicious: can you feed an overtone to a Rite? Ruling for the future: yes, and Rites should carry an overtone kicker ("if the banished components include an overtone, +2"). Sacrificing a 16+ card you raced to claim is exactly the endgame agony both boxes want.

  • Last Rites × Convergence. Real contact: co-op already redefines the endgame, so Rites need a co-op face (Rites as the final collective workings). Not a blocker — a chapter in Convergence's design, later.

  • Overtones × Convergence. Demand patterns may name overtone values as crisis-tier requirements. Clean, additive.

  • All four + Spell Duels: Duels is off in the Drought, so Last Rites never touches it. Schools' duel quirks are an explicit cross-sell hook (gated behind owning both). Overtones make premium duel ammunition — a 16+ strike is terrifying and wildly expensive to risk; flag for the Duels doc when Overtones is developed.

Line-level takeaway: Schools is upstream of everything (identity makes every later box richer and costs the others nothing); Last Rites is fully independent of the unresolved counter economy and of Duels; Overtones and Convergence are both downstream (need stable scoring / a working pipeline respectively).

Part 3 — Development recommendation: Schools + Last Rites first

Downgraded 2026-07-16 (decision 2026-07-16-expansion-status-reset): this was written as a choice; it is a recommendation only. No expansion sequencing is settled — Ascension Trials is the only locked-in expansion.

Recommended: The Schools and The Last Rites. Reasoning against the alternatives, not just for the winners:

  1. Independence from the two open wounds. The counter economy is mid-repair and the score table will move when it lands. Overtones is built out of the score table (masterwork tiers, claim conditions tuned to spell sizes) — developing it now means rework. Schools (leans are flat RP riders) and Rites (banish-for-effect, no counter contact) touch neither system's contested core.

  2. Cost per learning. Schools = one card per player; Rites = ~8 cards + markers. Both are printable in an afternoon. Convergence needs difficulty curves across five player counts — a different discipline and 10× the tuning sessions.

  3. They answer different questions, so testing both is parallel not redundant: Schools tests does asymmetric identity fit this game at all (a strategic question), Rites tests can the Drought carry content (a structural question). Overtones and Convergence each partially depend on those answers.

  4. They're the two the current playtest cadence can feed. Sessions are 2–3 weeks apart; both modules can be validated in a single session each (Schools even piggybacks on any base-game test for free).
