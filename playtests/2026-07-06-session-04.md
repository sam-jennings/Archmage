# Session 4 — 2026-07-06

## Goal
Run the F1 "text vs structure" counter-comprehension test in its cleanest environment
(2 players, one genuine first-timer, written rules only). See the protocol:
`_review/2P_NEW_PLAYER_PLAYTEST_2026-07.md`.

## Hypothesis being tested
If a first-time player learns the counter loop from the written rules, they will perform
Cast → Recall → Learn correctly with ≤1 clarifying question and no silent Recall misplay.

*(Caveat vs the plan's prereq: no rewritten control-arm counter text existed yet — Denise
read the **current online reference/rulebook**. So this datum is "current online reference
text is comprehensible," not "the rewrite works." Echo cards were not shown; effectively a
clean 2–4p test.)*

## What happened
- Sam gave Denise the online reference/rulebook and explained **nothing**.
- **Counters: no comprehension problem at all.** Denise understood the counters' double
  purpose (cast, Recall, then spend again to learn) directly from the written reference.
  She even asked whether physical counters are needed at all "as long as you somehow
  remember how much capacity you have."
- **Counter availability felt right at +1/+3** (3-card Enchantment = +1, 4-card = +3).
- Early rules question: **"do you need a wild card to make a 5-card Enchantment?"** — asked
  despite never seeing Echo cards, i.e. the materials implied a 5-card Enchantment exists.
- **Overpowered strategy A (last-turn giant Reshape):** short on counters, during the
  Drought Denise added any new cards onto her Conjurations, then on the **final turn spent
  a single counter on one enormous Reshape** to restructure her Spellbook.
- **Overpowered strategy B (15-card Transfiguration):** she dominated with a **15-card
  Transfiguration**, which felt far easier to assemble than even a 13-card Perfect
  Transmutation. She built her **first Transfiguration on the last turn without planning
  it**.

## What worked
- The counter loop taught itself from the **online reference** — the dual-purpose mechanic
  that failed for players in Sessions 1 & 3 was grasped with no verbal patching here.
- +1/+3 Enchantment counter values felt correctly tuned to this player.

## What failed
- **Transfiguration/PT balance:** a max-size Transfiguration is both easier to reach and
  higher-impact than a Perfect Transmutation of nearly the same length — and reachable
  reactively on the final turn with zero planning.
- **Reshape economy at end-game:** one counter buys an arbitrarily large Reshape, letting
  a counter-poor player restructure their whole Spellbook cheaply on the last turn.

## What confused players
- Whether a **wild** can complete a **5-card Enchantment**. In 2–4p only four energies
  exist, so a 5-card Enchantment is impossible and a wild may not stand in for an absent
  fifth energy (F6 ruling). The materials still imply a 5-card tier exists at all counts —
  that's the stale implication that prompted the question.

## Best moment
Denise reading the counter rules cold and immediately using them correctly — then
challenging whether the counters even need to be physical. The mechanic that has blocked
every prior session was, for her, a non-event.

## Worst moment
The last-turn reactive 15-card Transfiguration winning the game — a high-value spell
assembled with no planning, outpacing the "harder" Perfect Transmutation.

## Player quotes
*(Paraphrased from Sam's notes)*
- On counters: questioned whether they're needed at all "as long as you somehow remember
  how much capacity you have."
- Early: asked whether "you need a wild card to make a 5-card Enchantment."

## Triaged feedback

### Keep (act on)
- **Transfiguration vs PT imbalance** — a 15-card Transfiguration beat a comparable PT and
  needed no planning. This is a directly observed design problem (behaviour, not opinion),
  and it recurs with the "easy big spell" concern. Re-examine Transfiguration scaling/
  scoring relative to PT and the effort each requires.

  cheap, high-leverage exploit for a counter-poor player. Evaluate Reshape scope/cost,

## Diagnosis

**Observed:** A first-time player learned the dual-purpose counter loop from the **online
reference** with zero explanation and no misplay, found +1/+3 well-tuned, but (a) asked how
to make a 5-card Enchantment in a 4-energy game, (b) won via a reactive 15-card
Transfiguration, and (c) exploited a single end-turn Reshape.

**Expected:** Counter comprehension was the open F1 risk; big spells were expected to require
planning; a 5-card Enchantment should not be implied at 2–4p.

**Mismatch — counters:** the counter mechanic/text was *not* the failure mode for this
player. The Session 1 & 3 failures were "couldn't understand from **card or board**" — Denise
used the **online reference** and had no issue. This points at the **printed reference card /
board legibility** as the real cause of prior counter confusion, not the rule or the
mechanic. (Single data point, different player — don't over-generalise, but it's a strong
reframe.)

**Mismatch — big spells:** Transfiguration's "any-energy sequence" reaches size 15 far more
easily than PT's "same-energy sequence," yet scores/impacts comparably or better, and can be
assembled reactively. Reshape being a single action regardless of size compounds this at the
end of the game.

**Likely cause:** (1) counter confusion is a *presentation* problem localised to the printed
card/board, not the mechanic; (2) Transfiguration difficulty is mispriced against PT; (3)
Reshape cost is decoupled from its magnitude.

**Decision:** No design changes committed this session (observations only). Next moves are
captured as routed ACTIONs below; the printed-reference-card redesign (existing P1) gains
weight as the true fix for counter confusion.

## Addendum — Sam's further notes (added 2026-07-08)

Additional recollections from the same session (2 rounds played with Denise), captured
after the fact.

### Enjoyment / replay / market signal
- Denise **liked it and asked to play a second round** (behavioural replay signal, stronger
  than a verbal compliment).
- She said she **would buy it for her family if it were produced** (purchase-intent signal).
- Caveat (product-viability audit): Denise is a friend, not a stranger, and compliments to
  the designer are weak evidence — the *behaviour* (second round, would-buy) is the real
  datum, and it is still non-stranger data.

### Two-round interaction dynamics
- **Round 1: no conflict.** Both players built their Spellbooks independently — parallel
  solitaire, effectively no interaction.
- **Round 2: heavy clashing.** Lots of contested spells; Denise played a **Conjuration that
  blocked Sam from extending his Perfect Transmutation**. Direct interaction emerged
  organically the second time.
- Open question from Sam: **would the game be more interesting with more players?** (More
  contention for the array / more blocking.) Feeds the pending 5-player validation.

### Transfiguration was only ever an end-game Reshape target (contradiction resolved)
- **Transfiguration was never a live, mid-game strategy.** Neither player learned or cast a
  Transfiguration during normal play; at 2p the array cadence lets you collect what you want
  often enough that Transfiguration never feels needed *while playing*.
- **It appeared only on the final turn, as point-optimisation via Reshape.** Sam Reshaped a
  **5-card PT into a 7-card Transfiguration and gained +5 points** — a jump he found
  counter-intuitive (he'd expected a same-ish-size PT and Transfiguration to score similarly).
  Seeing that, Denise Reshaped into the **15-card Transfiguration** that won the game.
- **Resolution of the earlier apparent conflict:** the main-body claim ("dominated with a
  15-card Transfiguration, built reactively on the last turn with no planning") and the
  addendum claim ("never cast in either round") are **both true and describe the same event** —
  Transfiguration is not something you build *through* the game, it's what an unbounded
  final-turn Reshape lets you *convert into* for a scoring spike. No mislabel; nothing to retract.
- **Consequence — the two P1s are one mechanism.** "Transfiguration vs PT balance" and
  "End-game Reshape economy" are not independent: the unbounded 1-counter Reshape is what
  makes a large Transfiguration free at end-game, and the score table then pays it more than a
  hard-won PT. The scoring assumes a Transfiguration was *hard to build*, but Reshape bypasses
  that difficulty entirely on the last turn. Treat them as a single coupled fix, not two.
- **Concrete mispricing datum:** 5-card PT → 7-card Transfiguration = **+5 points**, against
  the designer's intuition that they'd be close. This is exactly a difficulty→reward ordering
  question: if PT is meant to be the harder spell, a 2-cards-larger Transfiguration should not
  leap 5 points ahead of it. Feed into the scoring-EV analysis.

### Strategic depth
- Denise described it as a **nice chill game to play while chatting** — enjoyable but
  **without the strategic depth of something like Catan.**
- Sam **wants a stronger strategic layer** but is unsure how much room the base game has for
  it, and wants to **explore added depth primarily through expansions** rather than
  overloading the base loop. ACTION(queued) below.
